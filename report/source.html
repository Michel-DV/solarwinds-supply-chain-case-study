Executive Brief

1. Incident at a glance

The SolarWinds incident demonstrated a strategic reality that now sits at the center of software-supply-chain security: an attacker does not need to compromise every downstream organization individually if they can compromise the mechanism those organizations already trust to deliver software.

Attack classSoftware supply-chain compromise / cyber espionage
Primary productSolarWinds Orion Platform
Build implantSUNSPOT
Delivered backdoorSUNBURST / Solorigate
Attributed actorRussian SVR; commonly tracked as APT29 / NOBELIUM
Public discoveryDecember 2020 following FireEye/Mandiant investigation
Do not read “18,000” as “18,000 confirmed compromises.” SolarWinds disclosed that fewer than 18,000 customers may have installed affected Orion builds. It later estimated that fewer than 100 customers were actually accessed by the threat actor through SUNBURST. The operation used broad distribution as a funnel and selective exploitation as the operational model.

Strategic significance

Orion was a privileged network-management platform deployed inside high-value environments. A malicious component delivered through a legitimate, digitally signed update inherited the trust already granted to SolarWinds software. The compromise therefore bypassed an important psychological and technical control boundary: defenders were not deciding whether to execute unknown code; they were installing an update from a known vendor.

The most important lesson is not that code signing “failed.” The signed artifact came from SolarWinds. The deeper failure was upstream: the build process that produced the artifact had been manipulated before signing. Authenticity and integrity were therefore evaluated at the wrong layer.

Core model: compromise the builder → manipulate the artifact → sign it legitimately → distribute through trusted channels → profile all exposed environments → promote only selected victims → move from malware-centric access to credential- and identity-centric access.

Scope & Confidence

2. What is known — and what remains uncertain

The public record is unusually rich, but it is not complete. SolarWinds, Mandiant/FireEye, CISA, Microsoft, U.S. government agencies and other researchers documented different layers of the campaign. A rigorous reconstruction must keep vendor estimates, government attribution, incident-response observations and analytical inference separate.

Statement typeHow it is treated in this report
ConfirmedDirectly supported by primary disclosure, first-party technical analysis or official advisory.
EstimatedScope or count published by an authoritative party but not necessarily independently verified for every victim.
AttributedActor attribution published by a government or established threat-intelligence organization.
Analytical lessonDefensive or offensive-security conclusion derived from the documented tradecraft.

The unresolved initial entry

Public reporting establishes that the attacker gained access to SolarWinds infrastructure before the malicious Orion updates were produced. The exact initial path into SolarWinds has been discussed through several hypotheses, but the public evidence does not support presenting one unverified path as settled fact. The security lesson is stronger when stated without overclaiming: once the actor obtained sufficient access to the build environment, they could manipulate the output of a trusted software factory.

Why Orion was a high-value target

Privilege

Network-management software commonly requires broad visibility and trusted access across infrastructure.

Position

Orion sat inside enterprise and government networks, often near identity, server and monitoring systems.

Distribution

Vendor updates offered a scalable route into many environments without repeating initial access.

Trust

Signed vendor binaries and expected network behavior reduced suspicion at installation time.

Red-team interpretation: supply-chain attack paths are trust-path attacks. The decisive question is not simply “what binary executed?” but “which upstream relationship caused defenders to allow that binary to execute with privilege?”

Timeline

3. Reconstructed campaign chronology

2019
Access to SolarWinds environment. The actor establishes a foothold sufficient to study and later manipulate the Orion build process.
Late 2019
Test activity. Public reporting and SolarWinds investigation describe earlier build manipulation/testing before the main SUNBURST distribution period.
February 2020
SUNSPOT build implant active. The implant is associated with monitoring the build process and replacing a legitimate source file at the correct moment so malicious code becomes part of the compiled Orion component.
March–June 2020
Trojanized Orion updates distributed. Affected builds containing SUNBURST are signed and delivered through legitimate SolarWinds update infrastructure.
2020
Selective victim promotion. SUNBURST performs environment checks, delays execution, beacons through DNS and receives instructions. A small subset of attractive victims is promoted to operational C2 and follow-on access.
December 2020
Discovery and public disclosure. FireEye/Mandiant identifies suspicious activity while investigating its own compromise, leading to the SolarWinds connection and rapid government/vendor response.
13–14 December 2020
Emergency response. CISA directs U.S. federal civilian agencies to disconnect affected Orion products; SolarWinds files its 8-K disclosure.
January–April 2021
Technical reconstruction and attribution mature. Detailed analysis of SUNSPOT, SUNBURST, identity/cloud tradecraft and post-exploitation is published. U.S. authorities attribute the operation to the Russian SVR.

Campaign design: patience over speed

The operation rewarded stealth. SUNBURST used a deliberate delay before initiating network activity, performed checks to avoid unwanted execution conditions, and relied on staged victim selection rather than immediately deploying noisy tooling everywhere. This created a separation between mass delivery and targeted exploitation.

Operational insight: broad exposure can coexist with narrow hands-on-keyboard activity. Counting infected artifacts alone therefore overstates confirmed compromise while simultaneously understating the seriousness of the selected intrusions.

The resulting software is legitimately signed and packaged as an Orion update.
5 — Customers install trusted update
Organizations deploy the affected build through the ordinary update path.
→

Dormancy, reconnaissance, DNS beaconing and C2 allow the actor to decide which environments merit further access.

Why the build layer matters

SolarWinds reported that its investigation found malicious code introduced during the automated build process rather than committed directly to the source-code repository. That distinction matters. Repository review, branch protection and source integrity can all be correct while the final artifact is wrong if the builder is compromised after source review but before signing.

ControlWhat it provesWhat it does not prove
Source reviewReviewed repository content appears legitimate.The build worker used only that content and was not tampered with.
Code signingThe artifact was signed by the expected signing identity.The artifact was produced by a trustworthy build path.
Hash verificationThe downloaded artifact matches the published hash.The published artifact itself is benign.
Vendor update channelThe update arrived through an expected distribution mechanism.The upstream producer was uncompromised.
Defensive requirement: treat source → builder → dependencies → signing → release → distribution as one continuous integrity chain. Attestation must cover the path, not just the endpoint artifact.

Malware Analysis

5. SUNSPOT — manipulating the software factory

SUNSPOT is the component associated with the SolarWinds build-environment manipulation. Its role was fundamentally different from the downstream SUNBURST backdoor: SUNSPOT existed to influence what SolarWinds built; SUNBURST existed to provide covert access once customers installed the altered update.

Behavioral model

  1. Persist inside the build environment. The implant runs with access sufficient to observe build orchestration.
  2. Monitor for the target build process. It waits for the expected compilation conditions rather than modifying every build indiscriminately.
  3. Replace the targeted source file at the correct moment. A malicious variant is introduced for compilation.
  4. Preserve build reliability. Operational errors that break production would increase the chance of discovery, so the attacker’s design prioritizes normal-looking output and build continuity.
  5. Allow the legitimate pipeline to do the rest. Compilation, signing, packaging and release transform the attacker’s modification into trusted vendor software.
Why SUNSPOT is the strategic center of gravity: once the attacker could reliably influence the builder, every downstream control that trusted the vendor’s release process became part of the attacker’s delivery infrastructure.

SUNSPOT vs. SUNBURST

SUNSPOTSUNBURST
LocationSolarWinds build environmentDownstream customer systems running affected Orion builds
PurposeInject malicious code into the build outputEstablish covert victim-side access and selection
AudienceThe software factoryPotential downstream victims
Operational valueScalable trusted distributionReconnaissance, C2 and access brokerage

Backdoor

6. SUNBURST — stealth, profiling and victim selection

SUNBURST was embedded in a legitimate Orion component and inherited the execution context of trusted SolarWinds software. Its behavior was designed to reduce immediate detection, learn enough about the environment to support selection, and communicate through infrastructure that blended into ordinary enterprise traffic.

Key technical characteristics

Dormancy

A substantial delay before network activity reduced the correlation between update installation and malicious behavior.

Environment checks

The malware examined host and process conditions and attempted to avoid environments likely to expose or disrupt the operation.

DNS-centric first stage

Victim information was encoded into DNS lookups under attacker-controlled infrastructure, creating a low-volume discovery and selection channel.

Staged C2

Selected victims could be directed toward additional command-and-control infrastructure instead of treating every infected environment equally.

Legitimate host process

Execution within the Orion software context made process-based trust assumptions less useful.

Operational restraint

The actor minimized noisy actions until an environment had been evaluated for value.

Detection lesson: “signed,” “vendor-owned,” and “expected process name” are context, not verdicts. Behavioral analytics must still evaluate outbound communication, parent/child relationships, unusual DNS patterns, credential use and identity events.

The selection funnel

StagePopulationAttacker decision
Affected buildPotentially thousands of installationsObserve environment while maintaining low visibility.
DNS beaconingHosts that activate and communicateIdentify organizational/network characteristics.
Promoted victimSmall, high-value subsetMove to operational C2 and follow-on access.
Hands-on intrusionSelected targetsAcquire credentials, expand access, collect and persist.

Post-Exploitation

7. Beyond SUNBURST: moving from malware to identity

SUNBURST should not be understood as the entire intrusion set. In promoted environments the actor could deploy additional tooling and, more importantly, transition toward valid credentials, remote access, cloud identities and living-off-the-land techniques. Once that transition occurred, the original Orion foothold was no longer the only persistence or access problem.

Follow-on tooling

Public reporting associated the campaign with follow-on loaders including TEARDROP and RAINDROP, as well as Cobalt Strike components in some environments. Tooling varied by victim, which is precisely why a response strategy based only on one malware family or one hash was insufficient.

Operational patterns that matter to defenders

  • Credential acquisition and use of legitimate accounts.
  • Remote services and administrative mechanisms that resemble normal operator behavior.
  • Lateral movement toward identity, email, management and other high-value systems.
  • Cloud access that can persist independently from the original on-premises Orion server.
  • Creation or abuse of service principals, applications, tokens or federation relationships where the victim architecture permits it.
  • Careful use of infrastructure and operational timing intended to reduce correlation across victims.
Incident-response consequence: deleting SUNBURST or patching Orion is not equivalent to eviction. If follow-on access produced valid credentials, federation-signing capability, service-principal permissions or other durable identity paths, remediation must coordinate endpoint, Active Directory and cloud actions.

Identity as the long-term objective

Management software can provide a privileged beachhead, but identity systems provide reach. A sophisticated actor benefits from trading malware-based access for access that uses credentials and tokens accepted by the victim’s own security model. This reduces dependence on the original implant and complicates forensic scoping.

Identity & Cloud

8. Active Directory, federation and M365 implications

The SolarWinds response highlighted a recurring problem in hybrid enterprise incidents: compromise can cross the boundary between on-premises administrative systems and cloud identity. Defenders therefore need to understand not just where code ran, but which identities, credentials and trust anchors were reachable from the compromised environment.

AreaRisk to investigateResponse priority
Privileged AD accountsStolen credentials, abnormal authentication, lateral movementReset/rotate in a controlled sequence after scoping persistence
Federation / token signingAbility to mint or abuse trusted authentication tokensRotate signing material and validate federation configuration
Cloud applicationsUnexpected credentials, consent grants, service-principal permissionsInventory, revoke and re-establish trusted application state
Mail / collaborationMailbox access, collection, forwarding or application-based accessReview audit logs and high-value accounts over the full retention window
Remote accessLegitimate VPN/RDP/admin use by the actorCorrelate identity, network and endpoint telemetry

Why coordinated eviction matters

Resetting one credential too early can alert the actor while leaving another persistence path intact. Mature incident response therefore plans an eviction window: understand access paths, prepare clean administrative identities, rotate credentials and trust material, remove persistence, block known infrastructure, rebuild where necessary and monitor for re-entry.

Architecture lesson: tier administrative identities and prevent monitoring/build/management infrastructure from becoming an easy stepping stone into the organization’s identity control plane.

Detection Engineering

9. Hunting and detection opportunities

Static indicators played an important role in emergency response, but the enduring value of the case lies in behavior and telemetry. Historical domains and hashes age quickly; the underlying attack logic remains relevant.

Telemetry priorities

TelemetryWhat to hunt for
DNSHistorical queries associated with Orion hosts, unusual encoded subdomains, low-frequency beaconing and resolver records retained across the exposure period.
Proxy / firewallUnexpected outbound communication from management servers, especially destinations or patterns inconsistent with normal vendor behavior.
EDR / processUnusual child processes, memory activity or follow-on execution from trusted management-service contexts.
IdentityPrivileged account use from unusual hosts, token anomalies, new credentials, service-principal changes, anomalous remote logons.
Cloud auditApplication consent, role changes, mailbox access and token use that cannot be explained by normal administration.
Build / CIUnexpected worker modifications, source/output mismatch, anomalous process injection, signing requests not traceable to approved provenance.

Detection design principles

  • Baseline privileged infrastructure separately. Management servers deserve stricter egress expectations than ordinary endpoints.
  • Retain enough history. Supply-chain campaigns can be discovered months after initial exposure.
  • Correlate across layers. DNS alone may be ambiguous; DNS + Orion host + identity anomalies is much stronger.
  • Do not exempt trusted software from behavior analytics. Signed binaries can still become execution containers for malicious behavior.
  • Preserve forensic evidence before destructive remediation. Rebuilding too early can erase the trail needed to determine whether the actor moved beyond Orion.
High-value detection question: what outbound destinations, child processes and identity actions are acceptable for each privileged management platform? “It is a trusted server” should produce a narrower baseline, not a wider exemption.

Impact & Response

10. Exposure, compromise and coordinated remediation

Understanding the impact funnel

The public narrative often collapses four different states into one number: customer of SolarWinds, installer of an affected build, host that communicated through SUNBURST, and organization selected for follow-on compromise. Those states are operationally different and should be reported separately.

StateMeaningResponse implication
Affected product/customerPotential exposure based on version/buildIdentify installations and preserve logs
SUNBURST executionMalicious component ranTreat host and surrounding trust relationships as suspect
Actor-selected victimEvidence of operational C2 / follow-on accessEscalate to full incident response and identity/cloud scoping
Confirmed post-exploitationHands-on activity, credentials or persistence beyond OrionCoordinated eviction, rebuild and long-term monitoring

Recommended response sequence

  1. Identify affected Orion versions and isolate or remove them according to authoritative guidance.
  2. Preserve endpoint, DNS, proxy, identity, cloud and remote-access logs for the entire possible exposure window.
  3. Hunt for evidence that the actor moved from the Orion process into additional tooling or valid-account activity.
  4. Scope privileged identities, federation, application credentials and cloud permissions.
  5. Prepare clean administrative infrastructure and an eviction plan before rotating critical secrets.
  6. Rebuild compromised high-trust systems rather than assuming malware deletion restores trust.
  7. Increase monitoring after eviction for re-entry through credentials or persistence missed during scoping.
Key distinction: containment stops current activity; eradication removes known artifacts; eviction removes the actor’s viable access paths. SolarWinds-class incidents require all three.

ATT&CK is a map, not the incident

Technique IDs help normalize behavior across tooling, but they should not replace sequence and trust analysis. The defining characteristic of SolarWinds was not any individual post-exploitation technique; it was the actor’s ability to enter through a signed, trusted supply-chain artifact and then selectively convert that access into conventional espionage tradecraft.

Defensive Architecture

12. Controls that raise the cost of a SolarWinds-class operation

LayerPriority controls
CI/CD identityShort-lived workload identity, least privilege, MFA for human administration, no shared long-lived credentials, strict separation between source/build/signing roles.
Build isolationEphemeral workers, hardened golden images, minimal network access, immutable infrastructure, independent monitoring of the build control plane.
ProvenanceSigned build attestations, traceability from reviewed source to artifact, reproducible or independently verified builds where practical.
SigningHardware-backed or isolated signing services, policy-bound signing requests, separation from general build hosts, audit logs for every signature.
ReleaseImmutable artifacts, signed manifests, independent verification before promotion, controlled update publishing.
Enterprise egressDeny-by-default or tightly baselined outbound access from privileged management servers; DNS/proxy logging with long retention.
IdentityAdmin tiering, service-principal governance, token/federation monitoring, separation of management platforms from identity control-plane privilege.
Response readinessPreplanned coordinated eviction, clean admin paths, credential rotation playbooks, preserved telemetry across endpoint/network/cloud.

What changed after SolarWinds

The incident accelerated industry attention toward software provenance, secure build systems, SBOMs, artifact attestation and formal software-supply-chain frameworks. Those controls are useful only when they reflect actual trust boundaries. An SBOM can describe components while a compromised builder still changes the artifact; a signature can authenticate a release while a compromised signing path legitimizes malicious output.

Design principle: no single control should be able to assert “this release is trusted” without independent evidence from adjacent stages. High-assurance pipelines need separation of duties plus verifiable provenance.

Red Team / Research Lessons

13. What this case teaches offensive and defensive practitioners

For Red Teams

  • Model trust paths, not only host vulnerabilities. CI/CD, package registries, deployment systems, management platforms and identity bridges can have a blast radius far larger than an individual endpoint.
  • Test detection on trusted processes. Defenders may over-allow vendor agents. Emulations should evaluate whether behavioral controls still trigger when activity originates from an approved management process.
  • Measure the privilege of software factories. Build workers and signing systems often combine code execution, secrets and distribution capability.
  • Emulate victim selection and restraint. A realistic advanced operation does not need to detonate every capability everywhere. Selective promotion is a useful way to test whether defenders can correlate weak signals.
  • Include identity/cloud follow-through. The meaningful question is whether initial access can be converted into durable access through the organization’s trust fabric.

For Detection / IR Teams

  • Maintain historical telemetry long enough to reconstruct delayed-discovery campaigns.
  • Define expected egress for privileged management servers and CI systems.
  • Build response plans that rotate identity and trust material in a coordinated eviction window.
  • Track artifact provenance independently from code signing.
  • Assume that a trusted vendor process can become an adversary-controlled execution context.

Study questions

  1. If an attacker compromises your build worker but never commits malicious source, which controls detect the change?
  2. Can your signing service prove what source and dependencies produced the artifact it is asked to sign?
  3. How long can you retrospectively query DNS, identity and cloud audit logs?
  4. Which management platforms can reach your identity control plane, and with what privilege?
  5. Could you rotate federation/token-signing material without losing administrative access during an incident?
Research takeaway: SolarWinds is a study in asymmetry. A carefully positioned compromise upstream allowed one actor to inherit the reach, reputation and distribution machinery of a legitimate software vendor.

14. Common myths, indicators and terminology

Common myths

MythCorrection
“18,000 organizations were hacked.”Fewer than 18,000 customers may have installed affected builds; that is not the same as confirmed follow-on compromise.
“Code signing was bypassed.”The malicious build was signed legitimately. The upstream build process had been manipulated.
“SUNSPOT and SUNBURST are the same malware.”SUNSPOT targeted the build process; SUNBURST was delivered to customers.
“Removing Orion removes the actor.”Not if follow-on access established credentials, cloud permissions, tokens or other persistence.
“SUPERNOVA was another SUNBURST stage.”CISA assessed SUPERNOVA as a separate malware campaign associated with a different actor.
